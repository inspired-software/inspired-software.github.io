About

A small studio that goes all the way down.

Inspired Software, LLC makes native software for Apple platforms. When the layer we need underneath it does not exist, we write that too — which is why the first app needed somewhere to keep a dive log offline, and a decompression model, before it needed a screen.

Why it exists

Founded to build the things we actually wanted

Inspired Software was set up so the work could be chosen rather than assigned. It exists to pursue passion projects — the ones worth years of evenings, whether or not anybody identified a market for them first.

A dive log with a proper editor inside it is not a gap somebody spotted in a category. It is what happens when the person writing the code also dives, also photographs, and got tired of keeping the logbook in one app and the photographs in another that knew nothing about depth, or water, or which fish that was. Everything on this site follows from building for that person first.

The work

Native software, and the layers underneath it

Each app is written for the device it runs on, and behaves the way the rest of that device does.

In practice that means three separate apps rather than one. Two of them are in the first release: the full editor on iPhone, iPad, Mac and Apple Vision Pro; and Apple TV, pared back to shelves, galleries and an auto-advancing slideshow built for a sofa and a remote. The third, for Apple Watch, is planned to follow rather than ship in the first release, and there is no date for it. It is built and tested — a dive computer first, with the synced log one swipe away. It also means the work does not stop at the interface. A dive log needs somewhere to keep dives, photographs and positions that syncs through your own iCloud and carries on working with no signal at all. A watch dive computer needs a decompression model. Pulling the veil out of a wide-angle shot needs a dehaze tool built for the haze water actually produces, which is not the haze in a photograph of a mountain. The libraries under all of it are in-house, written here and maintained here.

Our first app is Deeplife, a dive log for iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple Vision Pro and Apple TV, with Apple Watch and an Android version both planned to follow. It is in active development and has not been released: there is no App Store listing, no public beta, and no date. Other work is under way that we are not ready to talk about.

How it’s built Meet Deeplife

Where your dives live

A stance, not an accident

Running a server would be the easy path. It would also mean keeping a copy of your dives, your photographs and your positions on a disk we control, and asking you to make an account to reach them.

So the copy on your device is the real one — a single file holding the log, with the photographs stored beside it — and it syncs into your own iCloud account. There is nothing of ours in between: no service to sign up for, no copy on our hardware, nothing of ours to go down. On a liveaboard with no signal, the app does not notice.

The same reasoning runs through the rest of it. Species suggestions are worked out on your own device. There is no trained model yet, so nothing is suggested today; either way, no photograph is uploaded anywhere to be looked at. Exported images strip their GPS coordinates unless you ask for them to stay. Sharing a dive goes through iCloud to the person you chose, who can look but not edit, and not through us.

Where we stand

Pay once, and it does not vanish when we do

Two things we intend about how our software is sold, and what happens to it if we ever stop. They are here because they are easier to say now than to be argued out of later.

A licence you keep

We would rather sell software than rent it. That is the intention for everything we make, Deeplife included: a licence you buy once and keep. No monthly fee to open your own dive log, and no feature you already paid for moving behind a tier later. It carries on working whether or not we are still here — there is no account, and nothing in the app checks in with us, because there is nothing of ours to check in with.

Lifetime, if you want the word for it, means the app’s life — not yours, and not ours: you go on using what you bought for as long as it runs, and the fixes and improvements we make to it are included. If some later version is ever a big enough piece of new work to stand on its own, we will say so plainly, and the copy you already have will carry on regardless. What we will not do is quietly restart the meter on something you have already paid for.

Running no servers is what keeps that honest: no recurring cost on our side that has to become a recurring cost on yours. There is no price yet and we are not going to guess at one before the app is finished — this is about how it will be sold, not what it will cost.

If we stop, the code goes out

If we ever stop a product for good — off sale with nothing of ours replacing it, or the company itself winding up — we publish the code we wrote for it under an open licence: a real one, that lets anyone use it, change it and ship it. Software you paid for should not become unusable because the people who made it moved on.

That happens as part of stopping rather than as a favour afterwards, and if it is the company that ends, the code goes out before the lights do. It covers the in-house libraries underneath the app as well, because code nobody can build is not much of a gift. And it survives a sale: if the product or the company ever changes hands, this goes with it. A release running late is not stopping, and neither is replacing an old version with one we still look after. Today none of it is published: the libraries under the app are ours and internal, and this is about what happens if we stop, not something to go and download now.

One honest limit: other people’s work stays under the licence it came with. The reference photographs and the wreck scans are openly licensed already and travel on their own terms rather than ours, the seafloor charts are drawn from public surveys that carry their own, and anything we ever take under someone else’s agreement is theirs to hand on rather than ours. The name and the artwork stay ours too. Everything we wrote, you get.

The fine print, which we would rather say than have you assume. This is a philosophy, not a legally binding promise. The formal terms of any purchase are whatever licence you agree to at the time, and nothing on this page adds to that licence or stands in for reading it. What is written here is the intent behind it, set down in public and in plain words, so it is easy to check and hard for us to quietly drop.

How we decide

We would rather say “not yet” than guess

Three habits that cost us features, and that we keep anyway.

  • Ship the honest version. Everything that turns a photograph into a species suggestion is written and in place — preparing the image, the model that does the recognising, the list of species it can name — and all of it runs on your own device. The model has never been trained, so the app says it cannot identify anything, rather than producing confident noise.
  • Say what a thing cannot do. The seafloor charts are drawn from global bathymetry grids, which resolve walls, dropoffs and channels well and small features rarely. The legend says so, on the chart, instead of implying a precision that is not there.
  • Read the licence, per item. Every reference photograph carries its author, licence and source page. The 3D wreck models take their licence from each model’s own metadata rather than from a blanket claim on a website, and anything we cannot ship under its actual terms is left out.

And the one that matters most. The dive computer is experimental and has not been validated for real diving. Never dive without a commercially certified dive computer.
