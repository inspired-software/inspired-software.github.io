Deeplife

The dive, and everything the dive produced.

Deeplife logs what you did underwater — site, depth, duration, water temperature, buddies, notes — and keeps every photo and video from that dive attached to it, behind a full non-destructive editor built for putting the colour back. Everything is kept on your device first and mirrored to your own iCloud account.

What it does

A dive leaves more than a line in a book

It leaves a record, a card full of photographs, a couple of videos, a species you only half-recognised, and a site you would like to find again. Deeplife takes all of it.

The log

A record you can actually search

Each dive carries its date, its site, coordinates, maximum depth, duration, water temperature, buddies, notes and a rating. Every word of the notes is searchable, and so are the rating and the favourite mark, so they work as filters on every device rather than only the one you typed them on.

  • Dive list with search and a favourites filter, dive detail with a stats grid and the photo gallery, then the editors — one split view on iPhone, iPad, Mac and Vision Pro.
  • Link a dive to a named site and it inherits everything the directory knows about that place, including which species are likely there.
  • Each dive’s coordinates are stored so they can be searched by distance, which is what makes “dives near here” answer at once rather than after a walk through the whole log.
  • Photos belong to the dive: delete a dive and its photographs go with it, rather than being left behind with nothing to belong to.
  • All of it lives in the private half of your own iCloud — your account, not ours — in a compartment of its own that holds nothing but Deeplife’s dives.

Photos & video

Thumbnails travel; the full frame comes when you ask for it

Every photo and video carries its own thumbnail or poster frame, so grids, shelves and the watch are instant on a device that has never downloaded a single original. The full-size image or movie stays in iCloud until you open it, then comes down on demand with a progress indicator, the way a file in iCloud Drive does.

  • Importing keeps two copies: one at roughly 2048 px as the full-size photo, and a thumbnail at about 360 px that travels with the dive so it is on every device immediately.
  • The camera details are read out of the original file as it comes in — body and lens, focal length and its 35 mm equivalent, aperture, shutter, ISO, flash, original dimensions, GPS — because the copy Deeplife stores is a freshly written image and would otherwise arrive with none of that attached.
  • Each photo also carries an editable depth, prefilled automatically when a dive camera wrote depth as GPS altitude below sea level.
  • Video takes the same route: crop, straighten, rotate and the whole colour grade — curve and mixer included — are applied to each frame as it plays, edited from a still-frame preview in the same panel editor. The clip you shot is never rewritten.
  • Video is set up for species suggestions the same way a photograph is — frames sampled across the clip, the strongest frame’s answer used, feeding the same suggested tags a photograph would. Nothing is suggested until a trained model is installed, and there is not one yet.
  • Export stills as JPEG or HEIC with quality, size and keep-or-strip metadata options — GPS stays private by default — or video as MP4 with the crop and grade baked into every frame. Single items or batches, handed to the system share sheet.

Full edit mode

A darkroom that understands water

The editor is the one you would expect from a desktop photo application, not a strip of filters. A live histogram with clipping indicators sits above collapsible panels: Light, with real whites and blacks endpoints; Colour, with a white-balance eyedropper; an interactive point tone curve; an eight-band HSL mixer; Effects; Detail.

  • Point tone curve with per-channel RGB curves, and an eight-band HSL colour mixer for the hue that only ever goes wrong underwater.
  • White balance as a source cast — temperature and tint — with an eyedropper for sampling a neutral out of the frame.
  • Exposure, contrast, saturation, vibrance and shadow/highlight, all stored as parameters rather than pixels.
  • A heal tool for backscatter: tap a spot, drag its source circle. Spots are anchored to the original frame, so they stay on the particle they were covering even after you recrop.
  • Crop, quarter-turn rotate, horizontal flip and straighten to ±15°, auto-zoomed to the largest inscribed rectangle so there are never empty corners. Presets: Full, Square, 4:3, 3:2, 16:9.
  • Underwater presets to start from — Shallow Reef, Deep Blue, Green Water, Night Dive — and press-and-hold to compare against the untouched original.
  • Auto grade estimates how much water the light travelled through from how the colours cut out, and consults your logged depth only when the scene has too little natural colour to trust its own reading. A footnote says which signals decided.
  • Remove Grade always returns the original, because the original was never modified.

Local work and the finishing pass

Dehaze is ours, and it was written for water

Haze in water is not haze in air, so this one is ours rather than borrowed from a landscape tool. It measures the colour of the veil the water hangs between you and the subject — read off the brightest one percent of the haze itself, on a small preview — then works out how much of that veil sits in front of each part of the frame, smooths that estimate, and applies it back at full size with the edges of the subject left where they were.

  • The finishing order is fixed: dehaze, then noise reduction, then sharpening, then vignette. Noise reduction runs before sharpening so grain is not amplified; dehaze runs first because it amplifies both.
  • Masked local adjustments target part of the frame — radial and linear gradients with draggable handles, or the subject picked out from its background automatically, on your own device — with a red overlay while you work.
  • Masks and heal spots are anchored to the original frame, so recropping does not drag them out of place.
  • The full edit applies in one fixed order — retouch, geometry, grade, locals, finish — and the photograph you imported is never written to.
  • Presets you save sync to every device you own.
  • One photo’s look can be applied to the whole dive in a single step, while crop, masks and heal spots stay per-photo, because those are about one frame and nothing else.
  • On a device that cannot run the fast version of dehaze, a simpler local-contrast one takes over. The tool does not vanish and no photo is left half-edited.

The Library

Every photo you have taken underwater, in one grid

The Library cuts across dives: every photo and video in the log at once, filterable by rating, by species, or by media kind. It is built for the job you actually do the evening after a trip, which is culling.

  • A selection mode for batch rating and batch deletion, so 200 frames from one wall do not need 200 taps.
  • A side-by-side compare view for near-duplicates, with each pane rateable in place.
  • Card import files each photo and video into the right dive automatically by capture time — dive start plus duration, with slack at the surface on either side.
  • Star ratings sync and can be filtered on anywhere, so a filter set on the Mac is the same filter on the iPad.
  • Species tags work the same way, which is what makes “everything I have ever photographed of this species” a single tap.

Marine life

120 species, and suggestions that will admit what they do not know

The field guide covers 120 species divers actually see, each with one openly-licensed reference photograph, a depth range, rarity, hazard notes and a description written for a diver rather than a taxonomist. Tagging is manual today: a tag you confirm stays attached to the photograph and follows it to every device, and you can filter on it, so every photo you have of a species is one tap away.

  • Reference photographs are public domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA, with author, licence and source page recorded per image and shown in the app — the About window on the Mac, the settings pane on iPhone, iPad and Vision Pro.
  • A searchable, categorised species picker, with a “Likely Here” section ranked by where the dive actually was.
  • A separate training set of 774 commercial-use images across all 120 species lives outside the app, community-verified and visually vetted, each labelled with taxonomy, category and geography.
  • Recognition, when it arrives, will happen on the device in your hands rather than on someone’s server. Everything around it — preparing the photo, matching an answer back to the field guide, loading a trained model — is written and tested. What is missing is the training itself, so the app declares the feature unavailable rather than guessing. An untrained model would produce confident nonsense, and confident nonsense is worse than nothing.
  • Once a trained model exists, suggestions will sit on the photo as pending tags you accept or dismiss. They sync, so the reviewing can happen on whichever device you are holding.
  • Linking the dive to a site tells a suggestion where it was taken: what a site is known for counts for more than what its destination is known for, which counts for more than merely being in the right region — and a species that does not belong in that climate at all is pushed right down.
On your hardware. Picking a subject out from its background and every colour operation run on the device in your hands, and species suggestions will do the same once there is a model to make them. No photograph goes to us, or to anyone else, to be examined.

Dive sites

770+ named sites, and what each one asks of you

The directory covers 300 destinations in more than 100 countries across 23 regions, from the Antarctic Peninsula to the Yucatán: reefs, walls, wrecks, muck, caverns, pinnacles, drifts and quarries. Freshwater realms are flagged as such, because the marine field guide does not apply in a cenote or under ice.

  • Proximity search: everything within 25–500 km of a city, island or dive town — geocoded through Apple Maps — or of any destination already in the directory, ranked nearest first with distances in your own units.
  • Every site page lists the other nearby sites within day-boat range.
  • Favourite the sites worth remembering, pin the handful you are planning around. Both sync to every device you own and surface as Pinned and Favourites at the top of the directory.
  • A Dived filter narrows the whole directory to sites you have actually logged a dive at, and every row shows its dive count.
  • Training tiers against the 40 m recreational limit: Recreational (705), Rec & Tec (59 — divable on a sport ticket, but you cannot reach the bottom on one) and Technical (14, down to 70 m). Badged in the list and on the site page, and filterable from the toolbar.
  • A separate depth filter works from each site’s charted shallowest and deepest points against the certification bands — 18, 30, 40, 55, 70, 100 m. By default it shows anything whose top you can reach; Whole Site Within Limit narrows that to sites whose bottom is inside your limit too.
  • Each site carries its location, character, kind and signature species — which is also what will tell a species suggestion where you were standing.

Wrecks in 3D

70 Great Lakes wrecks you can turn over in your hands

Seventy Great Lakes wrecks carry a photogrammetry model on their site page — spin it, zoom it, expand it to fill the pane. The licences were read model by model from the publisher’s own records rather than the blanket statement on their website, which the individual models disagree with in both directions.

  • The 30 under CC BY are held on the device itself: they spin with no connection at all, and on Vision Pro the wreck stands in the room with you.
  • The rest stream through the publisher’s own viewer, unmodified, because CC BY-ND permits a faithful format conversion but not redistributing a reduced one.
  • Attribution appears under the viewer, in the in-app acknowledgements, and model by model in the written credits kept with the project. The wreck descriptions came from the same organisation’s research, so the site page credits that text too.
  • Models under non-commercial licences, or whose pages withhold coordinates or require third-party consent, are left out entirely.
  • Scans run to about 180 MB each, so they sit in Deeplife’s own storage on the device and are read from there when you open a wreck, rather than riding along inside the app download — which seventy of them would make enormous.

Seafloor charts

What the site looks like from the side

Every site page can switch its map from Apple Maps to a Seafloor view, both centred on the site’s own coordinates so that toggling never moves the view. The seafloor mode draws real bathymetry — an 8 km patch per site, from GMRT and GEBCO — as a depth-shaded chart with contours weighted toward recreational depths, a scale bar and the site marker.

  • The terrain ships inside the app: 716 patches, about 13 MB in total, compressed to roughly 12–20 KB each. No connection is needed to draw a chart, which matters on a boat.
  • It is already organised region by region, so the set can move to per-region downloads later — take the Red Sea and leave the Great Lakes — without the charts themselves changing at all.

Global grids resolve walls, dropoffs and channels well, and small features rarely. The legend says so, rather than implying a precision that is not there.

Sync & sharing

Your iCloud, your copy, nothing to sign up for

Everything lives in one file on your device with the photographs stored beside it, and mirrors to your own iCloud: the private half for your own log, a separate half for dives other people have shared with you. The copy on your device is the real one. We run no servers and hold no copy of anything.

  • Works fully offline. Changes push when there is signal again, which on a liveaboard is Thursday.
  • Edit the same dive on two devices while both are out of signal and the most recent edit is the one that sticks.
  • What a dive is — every piece of it, and every way of searching it — is written down once and enforced everywhere: on each device, in your own iCloud, and in a dive shared with you. Nothing can save a half-formed dive that another device then cannot read.
  • Share a dive and its photos with a link. Each shared dive gets a compartment of its own, and later edits are copied into it, so the person you sent it to sees the current version rather than the version as it stood when you pressed share.
  • People you share with can look, not edit. In this first version that limit is enforced by iCloud itself, not merely hidden in the app.
  • On Apple TV nothing is precious: its copy sits in space the system is free to reclaim whenever it needs it, and rebuilds itself from iCloud the next time you open the app.

Apple Watch — planned

A dive computer first, a logbook second

Not in the first release. The watch app is built and tested, but it is not part of the first release and has no date. This is what it does, written in the present tense because it exists — not because you can have it yet.

The watch app opens on the dive computer: Bühlmann ZH-L16C with gradient factors, from our own decompression library. On an Apple Watch Ultra it takes live depth and temperature from the submersion sensor, shows no-decompression limit — or the ceiling and time to surface once you are into deco — and raises ascent-rate, ceiling, deco and CNS alerts with haptics.

  • Gas (Air, EAN32, EAN36), gradient-factor conservatism presets and water type are set on the idle screen and captured at dive start; mid-dive edits apply to the next dive, not this one.
  • The diving screen is a depth headline, dive time, NDL or ceiling and time to surface, temperature, an ascent-rate gauge and an alert banner.
  • The session snapshots itself on every sample, so a crash mid-dive resumes with tissue state intact.
  • Surfacing is detected automatically; the summary saves into the synced log with one tap, and the log itself is one swipe away.
  • Afterwards it tracks the surface interval and a tissue-based no-fly countdown, simulating desaturation against an 8,000 ft cabin rather than running a fixed guideline clock.
  • Residual nitrogen carries into repetitive dives: the next dive begins with the previous dive’s off-gassed tissues and a correctly shorter NDL, and survives an app restart.
  • Battery safeguards: starting below 50% asks for confirmation, and crossing 20% and 10% mid-dive warns with a haptic.
  • Optional Apple Health sync writes finished dives as Underwater Diving workouts with depth and water-temperature series — opt-in, with write-only authorisation.
Warning. The dive computer is experimental and has not been validated for real diving. Never dive without a commercially certified dive computer.

Platforms

What each one is for

Each platform gets the part of Deeplife it is actually good at, rather than a share of one layout.

Platform Role Notable capability
iPhone Full editor (iOS 27) Log a dive, import photos and video, edit, tag, share. The whole app.
iPad Full editor (iOS 27) The same app, with the dive list, the dive detail and the panel editor side by side in one window.
Mac Full editor (macOS 27) The same again; the About window carries the field guide’s photo credits.
Apple Vision Pro Full editor (visionOS 27) The openly licensed wreck scans stand in the room in front of you, with no connection involved.
Apple TV Viewing only (tvOS 27) Shelves by recent, favourites and site, plus an auto-advancing full-screen slideshow that falls back to the thumbnail while the full-resolution copy is still transferring.
Apple Watch Planned Not in the first release. A dive computer with the synced log behind it — live depth and temperature on an Ultra, no-decompression and deco readouts, ascent and ceiling alerts, one-tap logging. Built and tested, but not shipping yet.
Android Planned Not in the first release. A version for Android phones and Wear OS is intended to follow — no date, and nothing to announce yet beyond the intent.

The editor on the phone is not a cut-down one. Same panels, same tone curve, same masks, same heal tool, on the phone as on the desk. The television and the watch are deliberately different: one has to be readable from a sofa, the other has to work through a wetsuit glove at 30 metres.

The watch and Android come later. The first release is the phone, the tablet, the desk, the headset and the television. The Apple Watch dive computer and an Android version are both planned to follow. We would rather ship the first release properly than put dates on the ones after it and then have to move them.
