Technology

Why it works where you actually dive.

Nearly everything interesting about how Deeplife is built comes down to two decisions. It has to work with no signal, because that is most of a dive trip. And nothing you put into it — your log, your photographs, where you were — should ever have to pass through us. This page is what those two things mean in practice.

With no signal

The copy on your device is the real one

Not a cached view of something that lives on a server. Everything you put in — dives, photos, videos, edits, tags, ratings, saved looks, pinned sites — is written to your own device first, and it is written there whether there is a connection or not.

That is not a technical nicety. It is a liveaboard with no bars for six days, a boat an hour offshore, a quarry in a valley, an aeroplane home. You can log the dive you just did, import the card, cull it, grade it, tag it, search your notes, look up a site and read its chart, with the aeroplane symbol on. When a connection turns up, everything you did goes up in the background. Nothing waits for permission to be recorded.

  • Small versions travel, big ones arrive when asked. Thumbnails and video posters live with the dive itself, so every grid and shelf fills instantly on any device — and so will the log on the watch, which is planned to follow. Full-size photos and movies download when you open them — with progress, and the thumbnail standing in until the real file lands. A phone does not have to carry a trip’s worth of full-resolution files to show you the trip.
  • The dive sites come with the app. 770+ named sites across 300 destinations, and the seafloor charts for them, are in the app rather than fetched. So is the field guide.
  • “Dives near here” is a real question. Dive coordinates are stored in a way that can be searched by position, so asking what you have logged near where you are standing is quick even with a long log and no connection.

Apple TV is the one exception, deliberately. There the local copy is treated as disposable — the system may clear it whenever it wants space, and it rebuilds itself from your iCloud, because an Apple TV should never be the only place a dive exists.

Where your log lives

There is nothing of ours in the middle

Your log syncs between your own devices through your own iCloud account. We run no servers, we keep no copy, and there is no account of ours to create.

So there is nothing to log into, no password of ours to lose, no terms to accept before you can write down a dive. There is no service of ours that can go down on the morning you get back from a trip, and nothing of ours holding your dives, your photographs, or the coordinates that would say where you like diving. We did not write a policy promising not to look. We built the app so that we have nothing to look at. The storage itself is your own iCloud, under your agreement with Apple.

  • It syncs when there is something to sync. After every save, and again whenever you bring the app back to the front. Records first, then the photo files behind them.
  • Both directions are yours. Your own log, and any dives another diver has shared with you, arrive through the same account. Nothing is routed via us on the way.
  • If we disappeared, your log would not. It is on your device and in your iCloud. Neither of those is ours to switch off.

Your photographs

Nothing is sent away to be looked at

Everything that examines an image does it on the device in your hands. The colour work. The estimate behind auto-grade. Picking the subject out of the background for a mask. The species suggestions, when there is finally something to suggest.

No photograph is uploaded for analysis, because there is nowhere of ours to upload it to. That is a consequence of the previous section rather than a promise bolted on top of it. Exports strip location by default, so a picture you hand to someone else does not quietly carry the site with it. Camera details — body, lens, focal length, aperture, shutter, ISO — are read at import and kept with the photo, because the stored copy is re-encoded and would otherwise lose them.

Where you were is evidence

A dive linked to a site knows a great deal about what you might have photographed. Deeplife uses four levels of it — climate, then the region, then the destination, then the site itself — and weights them accordingly. A site with a signature species counts for much more than a whole ocean does.

This works today, with or without a trained model, because the same weighting is what fills the “Likely Here” section of the species picker and the expected-life list on a site page. When there is a model, it reorders what the model thought — it never overrules it.

The editor

Built for water, not adapted to it

Water does three specific things to a photograph, and a general-purpose editor is only accidentally good at any of them. The tools in Deeplife exist because of those three things, not because photo apps usually ship them.

Water eats red first

Then orange, then yellow. And it does it by how much water sits between the lens and the subject, not by depth alone — which is why a close, strobe-lit subject deep down can keep its colour while a reef shot from well back in shallow water loses it. The white balance, the tone curve and an eight-band colour mixer are there to put back what the water took, band by band, rather than tinting the whole frame and hoping.

Backscatter ruins wide-angle

Every particle between the strobe and the subject comes back as a white dot, and a good wide-angle shot can carry hundreds. The heal tool is built for exactly that: tap a spot to remove it, drag the source circle if the app picked badly. Every spot is anchored to the original frame, so recropping the picture later does not slide your afternoon’s retouching off target.

Haze flattens everything far away

Past a few metres the water itself puts a veil over the scene and contrast collapses. The dehaze tool was written from scratch for that veil — the kind water makes, not the kind air makes. It measures the colour of the veil from the picture itself and pulls the scene back out from underneath it, keeping edges intact rather than smearing them. A test insists it widen the spread of light and dark in a deliberately hazed image by at least a quarter; it manages about 44%.

Auto-grade guesses, and admits how hard it guessed

One tap suggests a grade. It works out how much water the light came through from how the picture’s own colours have fallen away — red goes first, so the balance between the channels is the measurement — and scores its own confidence from how much colour variation is left to read.

The depth you logged only gets a say when it disagrees with the picture by more than 8 m, and even then only in proportion to how little the picture had to say. A colourless shallow scene defers to your log. A strobe-lit subject at 30 m keeps its almost-untouched grade, because grading it for 30 m of water would wreck it. A footnote in the editor tells you which of the two decided.

Video gets the same treatment, frame by frame

Crop, straighten, rotate and the whole colour grade — tone curve and mixer included — apply to video as it plays, edited in the same panels against a still frame. Nothing is re-rendered while you work. Exporting to MP4 is the point where it is finally baked into every frame.

Four underwater starting points come built in — Shallow Reef, Deep Blue, Green Water, Night Dive — and your own saved looks follow you to every device.

Non-destructive

Nothing you do to a photograph destroys it

The file that came off the card is stored once and never modified again. Every edit is kept beside it as a set of instructions, and re-applied when the photo is shown, on whatever device you happen to be holding.

Remove grade means remove grade

Not an approximate reversal. The untouched original is still there, so removing the grade hands it straight back, however many rounds of adjustment happened in between and however long ago.

A crop can be undone months later

Because heal spots and masks are anchored to the original frame rather than to the crop, changing your mind about the composition does not cost you the retouching you did on top of it. The spots stay where the particles were.

Opening a photo does not cost it quality

A photo with no edits on it is passed through untouched rather than re-saved, so nothing picks up the slow degradation that comes from being opened and written out again and again.

Because the edit is instructions rather than a new file, it syncs like everything else. A photo graded on the Mac is graded on the Apple TV and on the phone, and it is graded for whoever you shared the dive with too. The watch, planned to follow rather than part of the first release, will show the same look when it arrives. Saving a grade also refreshes the small version, so grids and shelves show the look you chose rather than the flat original — and one photo’s look can be applied to a whole dive in a single step, while the crop, masks and heal spots stay individual to each picture, because those are never the same twice.

On the wrist — planned

The watch does its own sums, and is not in the first release

Apple Watch is planned to follow, not part of the first release. The watch app is built and tested — the decompression work below is real and running — but it is not shipping with the first release and there is no date on it. It is described here in the present tense because it exists, not because you can have it yet.

Bühlmann ZH-L16C with gradient factors, computed on the watch itself, at depth, with no phone in the picture and no signal of any kind. Nothing about the dive you are on depends on anything outside the strap.

What it shows while you are down

Live depth and water temperature from the Apple Watch Ultra’s own submersion sensor. NDL while you have one, and a ceiling with a time-to-surface once you do not. An ascent-rate gauge, and alerts for coming up too fast, going through a ceiling, entering deco, and oxygen exposure climbing too high. Gas, conservatism and water type are set before you get in; changes mid-dive apply to the next one.

It does not lose the dive if something interrupts it

The full picture of your tissue loading is written down on every single sample, so an interruption mid-dive resumes exactly where it was rather than starting you from clean. It also notices you have surfaced and ends the dive itself, then offers the summary to save into the log in one tap.

Nitrogen that does not reset at the surface

Afterwards the watch tracks your surface interval and a no-fly countdown worked out from your own tissues desaturating against an 8,000 ft cabin, rather than a fixed guideline clock. Residual nitrogen carries into the next dive, which starts with a correctly shorter NDL, and it survives restarts.

Battery, and Apple Health

Starting a dive below 50% asks you to confirm; crossing 20% and 10% underwater taps your wrist. Finished dives can optionally be written to Apple Health as Underwater Diving workouts, with the depth and water-temperature traces, the same as other watch dive apps.

The dive computer is experimental and has not been validated for real diving. Never dive without a commercially certified dive computer.

Honest limits

What it does not do, stated plainly

Deeplife is unreleased and still being built. These are the things it is genuinely bad at, cannot do at all, or is not shipping with the first release, in the same words we would use if you asked us in person.

Species suggestions do not work yet

Not “work poorly” — do not work. Nothing has been trained, so the app declines to suggest rather than inventing a confident answer about a shark you photographed. What does work today is the field guide of 120 species and the ordering by where you dived. The 774 vetted training photographs are collected; your own tags are what would eventually finish the job. When a model does exist it will read video as well as stills — frames sampled across the clip, the best one deciding — and offer the same suggestions either way.

The seafloor charts are coarse

They come from global surveys, and global surveys resolve walls, dropoffs and channels well and small features rarely. A pinnacle you can swim around in four minutes may simply not be in the data. The legend says so on the chart itself rather than implying a precision that is not there.

Sharing is one-way

Someone you share a dive with can look at it and at its photographs, and they will see later edits when you make them. They cannot change anything, add anything, or write into your log. That is enforced properly, not just hidden in the interface.

Two offline devices, one dive

Edit the same dive on two devices while both are offline and the more recent edit is the one that survives. There is no merge and no prompt. It is a real limitation and worth knowing about before it happens rather than after.

The watch is not in the first release

The dive computer described further up this page runs on the wrist, works out its own decompression and needs no phone — and it is planned to follow the first release rather than ship with it. There is no date, and we are not going to invent one. Nothing else in the app waits on it: the log, the sites, the charts and the editor are the first release.

Your own dive computer, later

Pulling dives off a dive computer of another make, onto the phone or Mac, is planned rather than shipping. The part that knows how to talk to those computers is written and tested against simulated ones; the rest of it is not done, and we are not going to put a date on it.

Forty of the wreck models need signal

Of the 70 Great Lakes wrecks with a model you can spin and zoom, 30 are held in the app and turn with no connection at all. The other 40 are shown through the viewer of the organisation that made the scans, unmodified, because their licence permits an honest copy and not a reduced one. Those need a connection.
